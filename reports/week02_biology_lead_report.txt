Protein Sequence Sampling Project
Week 2 Report - Biology Lead

This week, I focused on understanding how sequence generation fits into our protein-design project and how I should interpret the results from a biological perspective. To build that understanding, my planned tasks were to work through an adabmDCA sequence-generation example, examine its outputs, and consider how available experimental measurements could support our evaluation. Building on the protein-family comparison from Week 1, I used the team's pilot results to connect the model's inputs with the sequences it produces. Through this work, I completed the small-example review for the Week 2 milestone, while the biological reference mapping and suitability of the real protein model remain open tasks.

The pilot used a simplified model with two positions and nine possible sequences, from which it returned 2,048 samples. The recorded checks showed that its sequence scores matched the reference calculations, giving me a useful starting point for examining the output. As I looked at the results, I began to understand why sampling produces some sequences more frequently than others: the model assigns them higher probabilities. For example, AC appeared in approximately 78% of the samples, close to its expected frequency of 77%. Seeing this connection made the process easier to follow and helped me understand why repeated sequences belong in the original results, since their frequency provides information about the model even if we later want a collection of distinct candidates.

At the same time, one of the most useful lessons for my role was understanding the limits of what this successful example establishes. Although it demonstrates a basic sequence-generation workflow, the synthetic sequences do not provide evidence about protein function. This also helped me develop a clearer understanding of model energy as a statistical score, since a favorable score alone does not establish that a generated protein will perform its intended biological role. As a result, I will need to keep this distinction in mind when describing our results and choosing experimental measurements to interpret them.

With these evaluation needs in mind, I carried forward chorismate mutase as the provisional biological focus because the earlier research identified a public trained model and a relevant sequence-design application. However, the source review also raised an important issue: some experimental labels had already contributed to developing the available model. Because those measurements cannot provide an independent test of its predictions, I still need to establish which measured sequences, if any, could support a separate evaluation. Working through this issue has helped me identify assay coverage and the history of the data as essential parts of choosing our benchmark.

Alongside these evaluation questions, the main challenge is connecting the simplified example to a real protein model without overlooking differences in sequence positions, gap symbols, or permitted amino acids. In particular, the chorismate mutase model's unusual parameter values and unresolved position mapping still need investigation. I therefore kept the biological model choice provisional and used the synthetic pilot to build my understanding while those questions remain open. Although the overall project direction remains the same, these checks need to be resolved before I can interpret generated sequences as candidates for the biological task. To help move this forward, I plan to raise the reference-mapping and assay questions at the next check-in so they can inform the benchmark decision.

Building on this week's findings, I will spend next week developing the biological sections of the proposal, explaining the motivation for the project, the provisional protein-family choice, and the limits of the available experimental evidence. Along with this writing, I will work on clarifying sequence numbering and the information the computer lead needs to apply biological restrictions correctly. Together, these tasks will help turn the initial pilot into a well-defined example that connects our software work to a meaningful protein-design question.
